Dart Turret Wiring

Power and signal wiring for the turret controller, with the main fuse, power switch and hardware arm switch. The low-current side is on two stacked controller boards from hardware/turret-pdb, a power board and a logic board; the flywheel ESCs stay on a quadcopter PDB. Matches firmware/turret/src/board.rs and docs/wiring.md.

Status: controller boards made by JLCPCB and assembled; the turret is not fully wired and tested yet. Controller boards lists every connector and where the boards, the PDB and the BMS sit on the frame.

Highlight

Power

  • Unplug the XT60 to turn the turret off. At 4S the spark from the ESC capacitors is small; plug in quickly and firmly, without wiggling.
  • The ESC capacitor inrush on plug-in can trip the BMS short-circuit protection. Plug in a few times on the bench. If the turret stays dark, the BMS has latched off: plug the charger into the pack for a moment to unlock it (see Charging). If it trips often, use an anti-spark XT60 with a pre-charge resistor.
  • Put the fuse as close to battery + as you can, on the pack side of the XT60, so it protects every wire after it.
  • The + and − buses are a quadcopter power distribution board (PDB). Every + pad on it is the same copper, and so is every − pad, so each lead in the table below can go on any pad of the right polarity. Soldered copper adds well under 1 mΩ and does not loosen from flywheel vibration.
  • Two SB560 Schottky diodes OR the battery (D1) and the 12 V bench jack (D2) into the bucks, so neither source back-feeds the other and the ESCs on the PDB stay unpowered on the jack.
  • The battery divider sits at J1, before D1, so it measures the pack only. Only the divided voltage goes up the link to the logic board, and C1 filters noise on it. On the jack alone it reads under 5 V, as "no pack", the same as on USB.
  • The divider sits on the turret side of the XT60, so its 0.36 mA does not drain the pack while unplugged.
  • Two stalled SC15s draw 3.4 A. U3 is an MP1584, rated 3 A peak, so it may overheat and cut out if both stall.
  • The MG996R pusher stalls at about 2.5 A and draws spikes that size each time it starts to move. C3, a 1000 µF 16 V low-ESR electrolytic at J4, supplies them, so they do not sag the 6 V rail or bounce the shared ground. U4 handles normal pushes with C3, but overheats and cuts out during a sustained stall.
  • With pole A open, C3 runs the servo for only a few milliseconds, far too short to push a dart, so the arm switch still cuts the pusher.
  • Set each buck before anything is connected, then lock its trimmer with a drop of glue. Above 7.2 V the MG996R is damaged.
  • Only signal and ground from the ESC servo leads reach the logic board: the middle pin of J5 and J6 has no copper, so a BEC's red wire stays unconnected.
  • Unplug the XT60 and the 12 V jack before plugging in USB, unless you have checked that the Freenove board isolates VBUS from its 5V pin.

Bench mode (12 V jack)

  • With only the jack plugged in, the bucks, ESP, gimbal and pusher have power, but the ESCs on the PDB do not.
  • Arming works as it does on USB, so the pusher can be exercised without flywheels.
  • A 12 V 2.5 A adapter (5.5 × 2.1 mm, centre positive) covers normal servo tests, but not a stall of both SC15s plus the pusher.
  • With only the jack plugged in, J1 should read about 0 V. That shows nothing back-feeds the PDB.

Controller boards

hardware/turret-pdb holds two stacked two-layer KiCad 10 boards, 70 × 68 mm, made by JLCPCB at 1.6 mm. Assembly steps, the stack tray and the safety checks are in hardware/turret-pdb/README.md.

  • Power board (bottom): battery input, 12 V bench jack, the two input diodes, the three MP1584 bucks hanging on its back, the battery divider, the arm switch and pusher connections with C3, and the servo bus and vision 5 V outputs. Every connection is a solder hole, as a plug would not fit under the logic board.
  • Logic board (top): the ESP32-S3 in sockets, the Adafruit 757 level shifter upside down between the socket rows, the light gate resistors and 2.54 mm headers for the ESCs, TTLinker, TFmini Plus and light gate.
  • Five wires about 35 mm long join the boards through J12 on both: 22 AWG for GND and 5V, 26 AWG for VBAT, ARM and PUSH.

Arm switch

The arm switch is a DPDT (6 lugs) or DPST (4 lugs) mini toggle. Its two poles are separate switches that flip together: pole A carries the pusher's 6 V, and pole B tells the firmware that the switch is on. Both must close in the same lever position. Pick the lever direction that means armed and label it on the bracket; the drawing uses lever forward, towards the turret, as armed.

  1. Fit the switch in the bracket with its two lug rows running front to back, so the lever throws forward and back.
  2. With a meter on continuity, flip the lever forward and find the two lug pairs that close, one in each row. Flip it back and check that both pairs open.
  3. Mark J3's four leads before trimming them, for example 2 red, 1 orange, 3 black and 4 white.
  4. Solder J3 2 and 1 across one pole and J3 4 and 3 across the other, as drawn. On a DPDT the front lugs stay empty; on a DPST each pole has only the two lugs found in step 2. Slide heat shrink over every lug.
  5. Before the first power-on, with the lever back, check that there is no continuity from J3 pin 2 to J4 pin 2 on the power board; with the lever forward, there should be. On power-on, status must show the switch on only with the lever forward.
  • J3 2 and J3 3 are 6 V and GND. Never put them on the same pole: flipping the switch would short the 6 V buck.
  • If pole B closes at the other end from pole A, the firmware reads on while the pusher has no power and off while it has. The last step catches that.

Servo bus

The two SC15s share one 3-pin bus from the TTLinker: 7.5 V, GND and data. Each SC15 has two identical connectors joined inside the servo, so chaining them puts both servos on the same three wires. The firmware tells them apart by ID, pan 0 and tilt 1, not by their place on the cable.

  • Pan servo first. The pan servo's body is fixed in the base, and the tilt servo turns with the turret. With the bus going to the pan servo first, one cable crosses the pan axis. Going to the tilt servo first would take two, one up to the tilt servo and one back down to the pan servo.
  • Slack across the axis. Tie the pan-to-tilt cable down close to the axis on both sides, with a loop between them long enough for the full pan travel. Size the loop with the turret in the middle of its range. Keep the cable out of the battery holder's middle tunnel, which the pan servo fills.
  • TTLinker on the fixed side, beside J7. It is about 33 × 21 mm and 8 mm thick with its connectors, too big for the 3 mm between Stack 2 and the tubes. Lay it flat on top of the right-hand tube, just behind the middle cradle and level with J7, on foam tape with two zip ties around the tube. Line its inner edge up with the inside of the tube so it does not overhang Stack 2; the rest sticks out past the tube. Its top is then about 51 mm up, under the 75 mm sweep. If the board allows, turn the servo port to face forward.
  • Short J7 leads. The data line from the ESP rises only through the level shifter's 10 kΩ pull-up. That is already slow at 1 Mbaud, and every centimetre of wire slows it more. Beside J7, the four 26 AWG leads need only about 8 cm. Keep them under 15 cm.
  • J11 can be long. Its two leads leave the power board at the front left corner. Run them across the front of Stack 2, in the gap between the stacks, to the TTLinker: about 12 cm of 20 AWG.
  • The servo cable takes the distance. The TTLinker drives the bus hard, so the long run is the cable to the pan servo: forward along the top of the right-hand tube past Stack 1, tied to the tube, then into the blue base. That is about 15 cm plus the way into the base. If the stock cable does not reach, use a longer one of the same type.
  • IDs. Both servos may come with the same factory ID, and the firmware has no command to change it. With the power off, plug in only the pan servo, power up and run ping 0; then do the same with only the tilt servo and ping 1. If one does not answer, set its ID with Feetech's FD software through a USB serial adapter, with only that servo connected, before chaining them.

Where it sits

The electronics go between the two frame tubes behind the blue base, in two stacks one behind the other, and stay below the top of the blue base, which the turret sweeps.

  • Stack 1, behind the base: the 4S BMS at the bottom on foam tape (61 × 46 mm, no holes), and the PDB on top on its 30–30.5 mm M3 pattern (about 36 × 43 mm with the battery tab). The PDB's board XT60 is left off, as the battery plug on top of the upright connector would reach into the turret's sweep. Instead a male XT60 pigtail on the tab's pads meets the female pigtail from the fuse and the BMS P− just outside the right-hand tube, each tied to the tube, so the pair stays within reach as the power switch. The balance plug from the BMS is tied next to the female pigtail, so the pack charges there without taking the cells out.
  • Stack 2, behind Stack 1: the power board at the bottom on four printed risers, buck modules down, and the logic board on top, 17 mm above it on M3 standoffs, about 41 mm tall in all, with the ESP antenna at the rear edge. The two boards are joined on the tray: the link wires stand up from the power board and are soldered on the logic board's front. Outline, hole and link pad positions for the holders are in the board README.
  • Both stacks sit in one printed stack tray that hangs under the tubes on zip ties; see the stack tray section of the board README.
  • The arm switch, a mini toggle, stands lever up in the middle of a printed bracket that bridges the tubes over the tray's rear cradles, so it is reachable from behind. Its J3 leads run back along the side of Stack 2.
  • The TTLinker lies flat on top of the right-hand tube beside J7, just behind the middle cradle; see Servo bus.

Battery pack

The printed holder takes the four cells as two stacks of two, lying front to back. Contact plates in the covers make the series links, so no wire runs between cells. The cells charge in place, through the pack-side XT60 and a balance lead from the BMS (see Charging), so they only come out to be replaced.

  • The BMS sits in Stack 1 behind the blue base (see Where it sits), so the pack leads and B1–B3 run from the battery covers out of the base to it. Strain-relieve them where they leave the base and again at the BMS, and keep them clear of the pan axis.
  • Pack leads and B2 leave through the hole in the back cover. The front cover has no hole: drill a 3 mm hole or file a notch for B1 and B3, and run them back along the frame.
  • The back bridge link carries the full pack current: 14 AWG, or nickel strip at least 8 mm wide and 0.3 mm thick (two layers of 0.15 mm). Lay it in the long channel of the back cover.
  • Solder to the plate tabs before the plates go into the covers, with the cells out. Nickel-plated steel takes solder with a hot iron and flux.
  • Let the cover slots locate the plates; test-fit a plate before gluing. Then fix them with 2-part epoxy on the edges and tabs only, never on the contact face or the spring coils. Avoid hot glue (softens near 60 °C) and superglue (cracks with cell swaps). Heat-staking the plate over a printed pin also works.
  • Strain-relieve every wire on the cover a few cm from its solder joint, with a zip tie through a printed loop or epoxy over the wire. Pulled wires are what tear plates loose.
  • The holder's pockets are 19.2 mm across at a 19.0 mm pitch within a stack, and its body is 65.0 mm long. The front cover's plate recess is 10.1 × 30.5 mm and 2.1 mm deep. With 65.2 mm cells, 0.3 mm plates and a back recess as deep as the front one, that leaves about 3.4 mm per cell for one button dome plus one compressed spring. Check the dome height plus the spring's solid height against that before gluing; if they do not fit, the cover cannot close.
  • Keep solder joints out from under the cells, as a blob under a dome or spring lifts the plate in its 2.1 mm recess.
  • After assembly, every spring should be visibly compressed, the covers should close without bulging, and the pack should read 12.8–16.8 V.
  • If the springs bottom out and the front cover only closes under hard pressure, print a shim from hardware/turret-pdb/tools/battery_shim.py (1.0, 1.6 or 2.2 mm, in PETG or ASA) and fit it between the holder and the cover. A 2 mm slit in each side lets B1 and B3 slide into the cell openings, so the plates stay in the cover. Pick the thinnest one that lets the cover seat by hand while every spring stays compressed. The original M3×12 screws still reach about 7 mm into the holder with the thickest one.
  • Mark + and − next to every pocket. A reversed cell makes the pack read about 8 V; the firmware then refuses arm and reports a battery fault.
  • Use unprotected flat-top or button-top high-drain cells. Protected cells are longer and limit the current far below the spin-up peak.
  • The 8 contacts in the chain are the weak point: at a 35 A spin-up peak, about 10 mΩ each sags the pack by almost 3 V. Use thick plates, print the covers in PETG or ASA rather than PLA, and measure the drop across a contact during a spin-up; more than about 0.1 V means that contact needs attention.
  • The springs are the worst of them. 18650 spring plates are sized for the cell, not for its current: they are made for torches and power banks drawing a few amps. Until a spring is fully compressed, the current runs along the whole coil, about 10 cm of thin steel wire, so one spring can be 30–60 mΩ. Four in series sag the pack by 4–7 V at the spin-up peak and heat each spring by tens of degrees per shot, which softens it until it no longer presses.
  • Measure every spring while the pack charges at a known current: one probe on the cell's − end next to the spring, the other on its plate. At 1.5 A, 15 mV is 10 mΩ and fine; 50 mV or more means the spring needs a shunt.
  • Shunt a spring with about 3 cm of 22–20 AWG silicone wire from its top coil, the one on the cell, to its plate. Run it inside the coil with enough slack for the spring to compress fully; the wire then carries the current and the spring only presses. Solder the top coil quickly, as heat softens the spring.
  • If a cell ever comes out, charge all four together in a standalone charger and put them back as a set. With the cells removed the BMS sees no pack and latches off; the charger unlocks it.

Charging

The BMS is common-port: the charger and the turret share its P+/P−, so a charger plugged into the pack-side female XT60 charges the pack through the BMS. The balance lead lets the charger measure and balance each cell, which the series pack needs once the cells no longer charge one by one.

  1. Unplug the XT60 pair. The turret is now off, so it cannot be armed while charging.
  2. Plug the charger's XT60 lead into the pack-side female XT60 and the balance plug into the charger's 4S balance port.
  3. Charge at 1–1.5 A in the mode that stops at 4.20 V per cell. On most hobby chargers that is LiPo; their LiIon mode stops at 4.10 V, which is also safe and gives a little less runtime. Never use LiHV (4.35 V).
  4. Unplug the charger and plug the XT60 pair back together.
  • Before the first charge, measure the balance plug with a multimeter: each pin should read 3–4.2 V above the one before it, from pin 1 (B−). The Gebildet board labels its tap pads 0V, 4.2V, 8.4V, 12.6V and 16.8V; 16.8V is B+ and the same pad as P+.
  • The charger stops the charge. The BMS overcharge cut-off at about 4.25 V per cell is only the backup.
  • Charge on a non-flammable surface and stay nearby.
  • The balance lead has no fuse. Keep its plug capped or clipped next to the XT60 so its pins cannot short against the frame or the connectors.
  • Before leaving the turret for more than a few weeks, use the charger's storage mode (about 3.8 V per cell).
  • The board latches off after a short-circuit or over-current trip, and when it first sees cells. Connecting the charger for a moment unlocks it.

Firmware battery guard

The firmware reads the pack every 5 s while the flywheels are idle, so spin-up sag does not count. It only sees the whole pack; the BMS protects each cell.

Signals

  • If ping 0 gets no answer, swap J7 pins 3 and 4. These boards do not agree on whose side the labels name.
  • The light gate phototransistor pulls GPIO 42 down while lit, and a dart blocking the beam lets it go high. The original wired it from 5 V, which can push more than 3.3 V into the pin; the pull-up to 3V3 avoids that. The IR LED also runs from 3V3, through 150 Ω, at about 13 mA.
  • The arm switch is the hardware arm interlock from the safety requirements. With pole A open the pusher has no power, so no dart can fire even if the flywheels spin. Pole B lets the firmware refuse arm while the switch is off and disarm when it is turned off.
  • The pusher signal goes through the shifter like in the original: most RC servos read 3.3 V as high, but some digital servos on 6 V are marginal and jitter. A BSS138 is fast enough for a 50 Hz pulse. Its 10 kΩ pull-up holds the line high until the firmware starts the PWM; servos ignore that as an invalid pulse.
  • The shifter's A3/B3 channel is unused.
  • The ESCs stay on 3.3 V signals, as in the original; BLHeli ESCs read them fine.
  • BSS138 level shifters are slow. If the servo bus shows errors at 1 Mbaud, use a 74AHCT125 buffer or lower the servo baud rate.

Vision node

The vision node is a second Freenove board with its camera (a GC0308 on ours) on the camera connector. It sits on the tilt stage directly above the barrel (see the sensor mounting section of docs/architecture.md). Only 5 V and GND run to it through the gimbal, from J10 on the power board; it has no signal wires to the controller, because it talks to the phone over its own Wi-Fi and to the controller over ESP-NOW in step 5. With Wi-Fi and the camera running, it draws up to about 500 mA, well within the 5 V buck.

First power-on

  1. With the ESP out of its sockets and nothing on J4, J10 or J11, check that VIN is not shorted to GND (an MP1584 fitted the wrong way round shorts it) and that the PDB's + and − buses are not shorted.
  2. With the arm switch off, check that there is no continuity from J3 pin 2 to J4 pin 2.
  3. Check the five link wires through the joints, as in the safety checks of the board README.
  4. On the 12 V jack only, set the bucks to 7.5 V at J11, 6.0 V at J3 pin 2 and 5.0 V at J10, and lock the trimmers. J1 should read about 0 V.
  5. With the cells in, measure the pack leads: 12.8–16.8 V. About 8 V means a reversed cell.
  6. Plug in the ESP. First battery power-on: arm switch off, no darts, flywheel motors unplugged from the ESCs.
  7. Run B on the console and compare with a multimeter across the pack.
  8. Run ping 0 and ping 1. No reply: swap J7 pins 3 and 4. Still none: check the IDs as under Servo bus.
  9. Run status and flip the arm switch; it should show on and off.
  10. Calibrate both ESCs to 1000–2000 µs before the first spin-up.
