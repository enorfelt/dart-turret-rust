Dart Turret Wiring

Power and signal wiring for the turret controller, with the main fuse, power switch and hardware arm switch. Matches firmware/turret/src/board.rs and docs/wiring.md. Status: proposed, not yet wired.

Two stacked controller boards for this wiring, made by JLCPCB, a power board and a logic board, are in hardware/turret-pdb. They change the power feed, the divider, the 1000 µF, the level shifter channels and the light gate LED supply; see Controller board, which also shows where the boards, the PDB and the BMS sit on the frame.

Highlight

Power

  • Unplug the XT60 to turn the turret off. At 4S the spark from the ESC capacitors is small; plug in quickly and firmly, without wiggling.
  • The ESC capacitor inrush on plug-in can trip the BMS short-circuit protection. Plug in a few times on the bench. If the turret stays dark, the BMS has latched off: plug the charger into the pack for a moment to unlock it (see Charging). If it trips often, use an anti-spark XT60 with a pre-charge resistor.
  • Put the fuse as close to battery + as you can, on the pack side of the XT60, so it protects every wire after it.
  • The + and − buses are a quadcopter power distribution board (PDB). Every + pad on it is the same copper, and so is every − pad, so each lead in the table below can go on any pad of the right polarity. Soldered copper adds well under 1 mΩ and does not loosen from flywheel vibration.
  • Without a PDB, use two Wago 221-415 lever connectors per rail, joined by a short 14 AWG jumper: each rail has six leads and a 221-415 takes five. Solder the 26 AWG divider wires to a buck input lead, as they are below the Wago's minimum. Do not use screw terminals on bare stranded wire; they loosen under vibration.
  • Solder the 39 kΩ directly to the PDB VCC pad and put the 8.2 kΩ and 100 nF next to the ESP. The 26 AWG wire then carries only the divided voltage, so a chafed wire shorted to ground draws under 0.5 mA instead of burning before the 25 A fuse blows. The 100 nF filters noise picked up on the long wire.
  • The divider sits on the turret side of the XT60, so its 0.36 mA does not drain the pack while unplugged.
  • Two stalled SC15s draw 3.4 A, so the 7.5 V buck must be rated 5 A. The original 8 V MP1584 (3 A peak) is too small.
  • The MG996R pusher stalls at about 2.5 A and draws spikes that size each time it starts to move. Solder a 1000 µF, ≥16 V low-ESR electrolytic across its leads at the servo end, after pole A: + to the red lead, − to the brown lead. A reversed electrolytic heats up and can burst.
  • With pole A open, the capacitor runs the servo for only a few milliseconds, far too short to push a dart, so the arm switch still cuts the pusher.
  • If an ESC has a BEC, leave its red servo-lead wire unconnected. Only signal and ground go to the ESP.
  • Unplug the XT60 before plugging in USB, unless you have checked that the Freenove board isolates VBUS from its 5V pin.

Controller board

hardware/turret-pdb holds two stacked two-layer KiCad 10 boards, made by JLCPCB at 1.6 mm; they can also be 3D printed with copper tape or wire tracks. The power board carries the battery input, the bench jack, the three bucks, the divider, the arm switch and pusher connections with the 1000 µF, and the servo bus and vision 5 V outputs. The logic board carries the ESP, the level shifter and every low-current connector. Five wires join them. Build, print and assembly notes are in hardware/turret-pdb/README.md. They change the wiring above in these ways:

  • Power feed: a single pad pair on the PDB (20 AWG) feeds the power board's battery input in place of the three buck leads (pad pairs 3–5). The ESCs stay on the PDB.
  • 12 V bench jack: the power board also accepts a 12 V adapter, for testing the gimbal and pusher without the battery. Two SB560 Schottky diodes OR the jack and the battery into the bucks, so neither back-feeds the other and the ESCs stay unpowered on the jack.
  • Divider: on the power board at the battery input, before its diode. It measures the pack only; on the jack alone it reads as "no pack", like USB. Only the divided voltage goes up to the logic board.
  • Pusher capacitor: the 1000 µF is at the pusher connection on the power board, not at the servo. The pusher and arm switch wires are soldered into the power board, as a plug would not fit under the logic board.
  • Level shifter: the Adafruit 757 is mounted upside down between the ESP's socket rows, with A1/B1 for GPIO 14 → TTLinker RXD, A2/B2 for the pusher and A4/B4 for TTLinker TXD → GPIO 21. GPIOs are unchanged.
  • Light gate: the IR LED runs from 3V3 through 150 Ω instead of 5 V through 330 Ω.
  • 7.5 V buck: all three bucks are MP1584 modules, including the 7.5 V servo bus. It is rated 3 A peak and two stalled SC15s draw 3.4 A, so it may overheat and cut out if both stall.

Where it sits

The electronics go between the two frame tubes behind the blue base, in two stacks one behind the other, and stay below the top of the blue base, which the turret sweeps.

  • Stack 1, behind the base: the 4S BMS at the bottom on foam tape (61 × 46 mm, no holes), and the PDB on top on its 30–30.5 mm M3 pattern (about 36 × 43 mm with the battery tab). The PDB's board XT60 is left off, as the battery plug on top of the upright connector would reach into the turret's sweep. Instead a male XT60 pigtail on the tab's pads meets the female pigtail from the fuse and the BMS P− just outside the right-hand tube, each tied to the tube, so the pair stays within reach as the power switch. The balance plug from the BMS is tied next to the female pigtail, so the pack charges there without taking the cells out.
  • Stack 2, behind Stack 1: the power board at the bottom and the logic board on top, 70 × 68 mm, about 41 mm tall, with the ESP antenna at the rear edge. Outline, hole and link pad positions for the holders are in the board README.
  • Both stacks sit in one printed stack tray that hangs under the tubes on zip ties; see the stack tray section of the board README.

Battery pack

The printed holder takes the four cells as two stacks of two, lying front to back. Contact plates in the covers make the series links, so no wire runs between cells. The cells charge in place, through the pack-side XT60 and a balance lead from the BMS (see Charging), so they only come out to be replaced.

  • The BMS sits in Stack 1 behind the blue base (see Where it sits), so the pack leads and B1–B3 run from the battery covers out of the base to it. Strain-relieve them where they leave the base and again at the BMS, and keep them clear of the pan axis.
  • Pack leads and B2 leave through the hole in the back cover. The front cover has no hole: drill a 3 mm hole or file a notch for B1 and B3, and run them back along the frame.
  • The back bridge link carries the full pack current: 14 AWG, or nickel strip at least 8 mm wide and 0.3 mm thick (two layers of 0.15 mm). Lay it in the long channel of the back cover.
  • Solder to the plate tabs before the plates go into the covers, with the cells out. Nickel-plated steel takes solder with a hot iron and flux.
  • Let the cover slots locate the plates; test-fit a plate before gluing. Then fix them with 2-part epoxy on the edges and tabs only, never on the contact face or the spring coils. Avoid hot glue (softens near 60 °C) and superglue (cracks with cell swaps). Heat-staking the plate over a printed pin also works.
  • Strain-relieve every wire on the cover a few cm from its solder joint, with a zip tie through a printed loop or epoxy over the wire. Pulled wires are what tear plates loose.
  • The holder's pockets are 19.2 mm across at a 19.0 mm pitch within a stack, and its body is 65.0 mm long. The front cover's plate recess is 10.1 × 30.5 mm and 2.1 mm deep. With 65.2 mm cells, 0.3 mm plates and a back recess as deep as the front one, that leaves about 3.4 mm per cell for one button dome plus one compressed spring. Check the dome height plus the spring's solid height against that before gluing; if they do not fit, the cover cannot close.
  • Keep solder joints out from under the cells, as a blob under a dome or spring lifts the plate in its 2.1 mm recess.
  • After assembly, every spring should be visibly compressed, the covers should close without bulging, and the pack should read 12.8–16.8 V.
  • Mark + and − next to every pocket. A reversed cell makes the pack read about 8 V; the firmware then refuses arm and reports a battery fault.
  • Use unprotected flat-top or button-top high-drain cells. Protected cells are longer and limit the current far below the spin-up peak.
  • The 8 contacts in the chain are the weak point: at a 35 A spin-up peak, about 10 mΩ each sags the pack by almost 3 V. Use thick plates, print the covers in PETG or ASA rather than PLA, and measure the drop across a contact during a spin-up; more than about 0.1 V means that contact needs attention.
  • The springs are the worst of them. 18650 spring plates are sized for the cell, not for its current: they are made for torches and power banks drawing a few amps. Until a spring is fully compressed, the current runs along the whole coil, about 10 cm of thin steel wire, so one spring can be 30–60 mΩ. Four in series sag the pack by 4–7 V at the spin-up peak and heat each spring by tens of degrees per shot, which softens it until it no longer presses.
  • Measure every spring while the pack charges at a known current: one probe on the cell's − end next to the spring, the other on its plate. At 1.5 A, 15 mV is 10 mΩ and fine; 50 mV or more means the spring needs a shunt.
  • Shunt a spring with about 3 cm of 22–20 AWG silicone wire from its top coil, the one on the cell, to its plate. Run it inside the coil with enough slack for the spring to compress fully; the wire then carries the current and the spring only presses. Solder the top coil quickly, as heat softens the spring.
  • If a cell ever comes out, charge all four together in a standalone charger and put them back as a set. With the cells removed the BMS sees no pack and latches off; the charger unlocks it.

Charging

The BMS is common-port: the charger and the turret share its P+/P−, so a charger plugged into the pack-side female XT60 charges the pack through the BMS. The balance lead lets the charger measure and balance each cell, which the series pack needs once the cells no longer charge one by one.

  1. Unplug the XT60 pair. The turret is now off, so it cannot be armed while charging.
  2. Plug the charger's XT60 lead into the pack-side female XT60 and the balance plug into the charger's 4S balance port.
  3. Charge at 1–1.5 A in the mode that stops at 4.20 V per cell. On most hobby chargers that is LiPo; their LiIon mode stops at 4.10 V, which is also safe and gives a little less runtime. Never use LiHV (4.35 V).
  4. Unplug the charger and plug the XT60 pair back together.
  • Before the first charge, measure the balance plug with a multimeter: each pin should read 3–4.2 V above the one before it, from pin 1 (B−). The Gebildet board labels its tap pads 0V, 4.2V, 8.4V, 12.6V and 16.8V; 16.8V is B+ and the same pad as P+.
  • The charger stops the charge. The BMS overcharge cut-off at about 4.25 V per cell is only the backup.
  • Charge on a non-flammable surface and stay nearby.
  • The balance lead has no fuse. Keep its plug capped or clipped next to the XT60 so its pins cannot short against the frame or the connectors.
  • Before leaving the turret for more than a few weeks, use the charger's storage mode (about 3.8 V per cell).
  • The board latches off after a short-circuit or over-current trip, and when it first sees cells. Connecting the charger for a moment unlocks it.

Firmware battery guard

The firmware reads the pack every 5 s while the flywheels are idle, so spin-up sag does not count. It only sees the whole pack; the BMS protects each cell.

Signals

  • If ping 0 gets no answer, swap the TTLinker RXD and TXD wires. These boards do not agree on whose side the labels name.
  • The light gate phototransistor pulls GPIO 42 down while lit, and a dart blocking the beam lets it go high. The original wired it from 5 V, which can push more than 3.3 V into the pin; the pull-up to 3V3 avoids that.
  • The arm switch is the hardware arm interlock from the safety requirements. With pole A open the pusher has no power, so no dart can fire even if the flywheels spin. Pole B lets the firmware refuse arm while the switch is off and disarm when it is turned off.
  • The pusher signal goes through the shifter like in the original: most RC servos read 3.3 V as high, but some digital servos on 6 V are marginal and jitter. A BSS138 is fast enough for a 50 Hz pulse. Its 10 kΩ pull-up holds the line high until the firmware starts the PWM; servos ignore that as an invalid pulse.
  • The ESCs stay on 3.3 V signals, as in the original; BLHeli ESCs read them fine.
  • BSS138 level shifters are slow. If the servo bus shows errors at 1 Mbaud, use a 74AHCT125 buffer or lower the servo baud rate.

Vision node

The vision node is a second Freenove board with its camera (a GC0308 on ours) on the camera connector. It sits on the tilt stage directly above the barrel (see the sensor mounting section of docs/architecture.md). Only 5 V and GND run to it through the gimbal; it has no signal wires to the controller, because it talks to the phone over its own Wi-Fi and to the controller over ESP-NOW in step 5. With Wi-Fi and the camera running, it draws up to about 500 mA, well within the 5 V buck.

First power-on

  1. With nothing on the buck outputs, set them to 7.5 V, 6.0 V and 5.0 V with a multimeter.
  2. Check that the + bus and − bus are not shorted, with the battery disconnected.
  3. With the cells in, measure the pack leads: 12.8–16.8 V. About 8 V means a reversed cell.
  4. First power-on: arm switch off, no darts, flywheel motors unplugged from the ESCs.
  5. Run B on the console and compare with a multimeter across the pack.
  6. Run ping 0 and ping 1. No reply: swap TTLinker RXD/TXD.
  7. Run status and flip the arm switch; it should show on and off.
  8. Calibrate both ESCs to 1000–2000 µs before the first spin-up.
